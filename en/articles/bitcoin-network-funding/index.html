Who funds the Bitcoin network as the block subsidy fades?

BitKultur · 8-minute read · Published: 27 November 2026

Bitcoin miners currently earn newly issued bitcoin and transaction fees. But the protocol gradually reduces the creation of new bitcoin. What will incentivise miners as this part of their reward becomes smaller?

This question does not show that Bitcoin is insecure today or that it will inevitably become insecure in the future. It is a reason to understand the economic incentives behind mining and the network’s security as related, but separate, topics.

What does a miner receive for a block?

Miners compete to add new blocks to the blockchain. Their computing work makes it harder to alter the transaction history. Under Bitcoin’s rules, the miner who creates a block may receive two kinds of income:

Block reward = block subsidy + transaction fees.

The block subsidy is the amount of newly created bitcoin associated with a block. Users attach transaction fees when they want their transactions included in a block; the miner who includes them collects those fees. Bitcoin’s developer guide defines the block reward as the sum of the subsidy and the fees.[3]

Halvings reduce the creation of new bitcoin

Every 210,000 blocks, the block subsidy is cut in half. This happens roughly every four years and is known as a halving. The most recent halving took place in April 2024. Since then, the subsidy has been 3.125 BTC per block. After the next halving, it will be 1.5625 BTC. New issuance therefore slows over time and is expected to reach zero around 2140.[4]

So the phrase “the block reward runs out” needs a qualification: it is the subsidy made up of newly issued bitcoin that gradually declines. The full block reward does not necessarily disappear, because blocks may continue to include transaction fees.

Why do transaction fees change?

A fee does not depend simply on how much bitcoin is being sent. It depends on the transaction’s size and demand for space in a block. Block space is limited. When many transactions compete to be included, users may offer higher fees to be confirmed sooner. During quieter periods, fees can be lower.[5]

That makes fee income variable. The halving schedule does not guarantee that transaction fees will grow at a predictable rate.

What do recent figures show?

A study by the Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance found that transaction fees averaged 0.67% of miner revenue in the 90 days up to 4 October 2026. The report also notes that fees have rarely exceeded 5% since 2010, and that substantial fee income has generally appeared during periods of unusually high activity.[1]

Hashrate Index’s weekly report dated 23 November gives a figure of 0.64% for the previous week: miners collected about 3,434 BTC in block rewards, while fees totalled around 22 BTC.[2]

The figures cover different periods, and the sources describe the denominator somewhat differently: Cambridge compares fees with miner revenue, while Hashrate Index compares them with block rewards. Both are snapshots of a particular period. Neither figure, on its own, proves that Bitcoin’s long-term security will be inadequate.

Miner revenue, network security and price are different things

A miner’s revenue is not the same as profit. Revenue must cover electricity, mining equipment, cooling and other operating costs. Those costs, along with bitcoin’s market price, affect how much computing power miners find worthwhile to maintain.

Network security depends on several factors. Bitcoin’s proof-of-work system makes rewriting earlier blocks difficult: the more computing work an attacker would have to overcome, the more costly such an attempt may be. Miner revenue is therefore an important economic signal, but it is not a complete security measure. Full nodes also independently check whether blocks follow the protocol rules.[3]

The price of bitcoin changes the monetary value of a reward denominated in BTC, but it is not the same as miner revenue or network security. These factors can be related, but one cannot automatically be inferred from another.

What might happen over the long term?

Bitcoin’s incentive model anticipates that transaction fees may play a larger role as the block subsidy declines. It is not yet known how predictable or sufficient fee income will be for miners. It will depend on future demand for transactions, competition for block space, energy and equipment costs, and bitcoin’s price.

The Cambridge study recommends monitoring several indicators together, including the share and volatility of fees, miner revenue, estimated costs of obtaining a majority of computing power, and mining concentration.[1] Long-term funding for security remains an open economic question. Today’s figures prove neither that a security problem is inevitable nor that it cannot occur.

In brief

  • The block reward consists of the block subsidy and transaction fees.
  • Halvings reduce the creation of new bitcoin; they do not automatically eliminate the full block reward.
  • Transaction fees depend on demand for limited block space, so they fluctuate.
  • Miner revenue, network security and bitcoin’s price are related, but distinct concepts.
  • How Bitcoin’s security will be funded over the long term is an economic question without a definitive answer today.
